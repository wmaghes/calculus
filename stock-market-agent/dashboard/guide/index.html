Individual Investor

Retail

Manages their own money for their own goals — retirement, a house, general wealth-building.

How they use the market

They open a brokerage account and buy stocks, funds, or bonds directly with their own cash, holding a single portfolio they're fully accountable for. Decisions are usually driven by a personal time horizon and risk tolerance rather than a mandate from a client or employer, and success is just "did my net worth grow the way I needed it to."

In practice

Most of the real work happens a few times a year, not daily: contributing to a 401(k) or brokerage account on autopilot, rebalancing once or twice a year back to a target mix, resisting the urge to do something rash after a big market move, and reviewing tax-loss harvesting opportunities each December. The investors who check and trade daily are usually the ones hurting their own long-run returns.

Variations on this role

Passive / index investor — buys broad index funds, rarely trades, and accepts the market's average return in exchange for low cost and little effort.
Active stock-picker — researches individual companies and tries to beat the index, taking on more risk and time for a shot at outperformance.
Buy-and-hold vs. frequent trader — some individuals trade constantly instead of investing for the long run; it's legal and common, but most underperform a simple index fund after costs.
Robo-advised — hands allocation and rebalancing to an algorithm for a small fee, splitting the difference between full DIY and a human advisor.

How they're paid

They're not paid — they're the client. Costs flow the other way instead: brokerage commissions (often $0 for stock trades today), fund expense ratios (as low as ~0.03%/year for a broad index fund), and advisory fees if they use a robo-advisor or human advisor (typically 0.25%–1%/year of assets).

Financial Advisor

Retail / mass affluent

Builds and manages investment plans for other people's money, tied to their clients' real-life goals.

How they use the market

Instead of picking stocks for themselves, they translate a client's goal — retire at 60, fund college in 15 years — into a target return, a risk tolerance, and an asset allocation across stocks, bonds, and cash. They rebalance over time, manage the emotional side of investing (talking clients out of panic-selling), and are usually paid a fee on assets under management rather than a commission per trade.

In practice

Client meetings reviewing progress toward a goal and talking through life changes (a new baby, a job loss, an inheritance), running financial plans and projections, rebalancing portfolios, and a lot of behavioral coaching — the single highest-value thing many advisors do in a given year is talk one client out of selling everything during a crash.

Variations on this role

Fee-only fiduciary — paid only by the client (flat fee, hourly, or a % of assets), legally required to act in the client's best interest.
Commission-based broker — paid by the products they sell (funds, insurance, annuities); a shrinking but still-common fee model with a built-in conflict of interest.
Robo-advisor + human hybrid — an algorithm handles portfolio construction while a human advisor is layered on for planning and hand-holding, at a lower fee than a pure human service.
Wirehouse vs. independent RIA — a wirehouse advisor (e.g. Merrill, Morgan Stanley) works inside a big bank's platform and products; an independent Registered Investment Adviser runs their own shop with more flexibility but more overhead.

How they're paid

Most commonly 0.5%–1% of assets under management per year (a $500k client pays roughly $2,500–$5,000/year), sometimes blended with flat planning fees. A commission-based advisor instead earns a cut of whatever product they sell — exactly the incentive fee-only advisors are hired to avoid.

Private Banker / Wealth Manager

High-net-worth

Runs several client relationships at once for wealthy individuals and families, blending investing with banking.

How they use the market

They manage a book of clients rather than one portfolio, each with a different risk profile, tax situation, and set of goals — and often a concentrated position in one stock (like a founder's or executive's own company shares) that needs careful, low-key unwinding. On top of investment management, they typically also arrange lending against a portfolio, trust and estate structures, and access to private/alternative investments not open to retail clients.

In practice

Juggling a book of maybe 30–80 wealthy households: portfolio reviews, loan requests against a concentrated stock position, coordinating with the client's own tax and estate attorneys, and a lot of relationship maintenance — this is as much a relationship business as an investing one.

Variations on this role

Private bank (e.g. Goldman, JPMorgan, Citi) — bundles investing with lending, trust services, and access to the bank's own alternative-investment funds.
Independent multi-family office — serves a handful of very wealthy families directly, with more customization and fewer product conflicts than a bank platform.
Trust company — focuses specifically on managing money inside trusts and estates across generations.

How they're paid

A mix of a base salary plus a percentage of assets under management they bring in and retain (often 0.75%–1.5%/year on the book), plus sometimes a cut of lending and other banking products sold to the same clients.

Investment Banker

Corporate finance

Advises companies — not individual investors — on raising capital and doing deals.

How they use the market

They don't manage a personal portfolio of stocks; they use the stock market as a tool for their corporate clients. That means taking companies public (IPOs), helping already-public companies issue more shares or debt, and advising on mergers and acquisitions — where a huge part of the job is figuring out whether buying another company will make the acquirer's own stock more or less valuable per share (accretion/dilution).

In practice

Long hours building and tweaking financial models, drafting pitch books to win new deal mandates, due-diligence calls, and — once a deal is live — coordinating lawyers, accountants and regulators to close it on schedule. Junior bankers mostly build models and slides; senior bankers mostly sell relationships and manage the process.

Variations on this role

M&A advisory — advises on buying or selling whole companies.
Equity capital markets (ECM) — runs IPOs and follow-on stock offerings.
Debt capital markets (DCM) — arranges bond issuance and leveraged loans.
Restructuring — advises distressed companies, and their creditors, through bankruptcy or an out-of-court workout.

How they're paid

A salary plus a large year-end bonus tied to the bank's and the group's deal volume — bonuses at senior levels are frequently multiples of base salary, the classic trade for brutal hours earlier in the career.

Key tools & concepts

DCF & comparable-company valuationPrecedent transactions Accretion / dilution analysisUnderwriting & deal structuring IPOs & follow-on offeringsPro forma modeling

Trader (Sales & Trading / Prop / Day Trader)

Markets & execution

Buys and sells constantly, either to fill client orders, to profit from a firm's own capital, or independently from their own account.

How they use the market

A sell-side trader sits between clients and the market, executing orders and often making markets. A proprietary or independent day trader instead risks capital on short-term views — on price direction, volatility, or the relationship between related instruments — over minutes or hours rather than years. This is exactly why the Simulator's speed control matters: a day trader needs to see a year's worth of price action in minutes to get enough practice reps, where a long-term investor genuinely wants real-time pacing.

In practice

Pre-market prep (news, overnight moves, the economic calendar), then intensely focused hours watching screens and reacting — a lot of it concentrated in the first and last hour of the trading day when volume is highest. Post-close is reviewing the day's fills and risk, and resetting for tomorrow.

Variations on this role

Sell-side flow trader — executes client orders and makes markets for a bank, paid on the business the desk generates.
Proprietary trader — trades a firm's own capital at a prop shop, paid a share of their own P&L.
Independent / retail day trader — trades their own account with their own capital, keeping 100% of the result (and 100% of the loss).
Market-neutral / stat-arb trader — tries to profit from relationships between instruments rather than outright direction (overlaps with Quant below).

How they're paid

Sell-side: salary plus a bonus tied to desk P&L and client revenue. Prop trader: a negotiated split of their own trading profits (commonly 10%–30% to the trader, more at senior levels), often with little or no salary at all. Independent: whatever they make trading their own money, minus their own losses.

Bid-ask spread & order flowPosition sizing Technical analysisLeverage & margin Stop-loss disciplineExecution algorithms

Quantitative Analyst / Quant Trader

Systematic

Uses math, statistics and code — not a gut feeling about a company — to find and automate a trading edge.

How they use the market

Instead of reading a company's story, a quant builds a statistical model of price relationships — two stocks that usually move together, a pattern in order flow, a seasonal effect — and lets an algorithm trade it automatically, sometimes thousands of times a day. The model is validated on historical data (a backtest) before ever risking real money, and speed of execution can matter as much as the idea itself. A strategy that works is called "alpha," and it tends to decay the more other funds discover and crowd into the same edge.

In practice

Writing and testing code, not reading news: building a hypothesis, backtesting it against historical data, checking it for overfitting, and — if it survives — handing it to (or building) the execution system that trades it live, then monitoring its real performance against the backtest.

Variations on this role

Quant researcher — focused on finding and validating new signals and strategies.
Quant developer — builds the infrastructure (data pipelines, execution systems, backtesters) researchers depend on.
High-frequency trader (HFT) — competes on pure speed, holding positions for seconds or less.
Quant portfolio manager — runs real capital against a portfolio of systematic strategies, responsible for the P&L, not just the research.

How they're paid

Base salary — often high relative to other finance roles, reflecting the software/math skill overlap with tech — plus a bonus tied to the strategies' P&L. A quant PM is typically paid more like a trader, often with a direct cut of profits.

Private Equity Professional

Ownership & control

Buys whole companies outright — often taking them private — rather than trading shares of them.

How they use the market

Instead of buying a small slice of a company on an exchange, a PE firm buys (or takes private) an entire company using a mix of investor capital and borrowed money (a leveraged buyout), then spends several years actively improving how it's run before selling it — often back onto the public market via an IPO, which is where it reconnects with the stock market this whole site is about. Returns are measured over years, not days, using metrics like IRR and multiple of invested capital rather than daily share-price moves.

In practice

Sourcing and underwriting new deals (financial modeling, management meetings, industry diligence), and for deals already done, sitting on portfolio-company boards and working with management on the operational plan. A junior PE associate's year looks a lot like a junior banker's — just with fewer, bigger deals.

Variations on this role

Leveraged buyout (LBO) fund — buys mature, cash-flow-generating companies using significant debt.
Growth equity — takes a minority stake in a fast-growing but already-proven company, with little to no debt.
Distressed / turnaround — buys companies in or near bankruptcy at a steep discount, betting on a fix.
Fund of funds / secondaries — buys stakes in other PE funds or their portfolio companies rather than doing deals directly.

How they're paid

A modest-by-finance-standards base salary plus "carried interest" — typically 20% of the fund's profits above a hurdle rate, split among the investment team — which is where the enormous PE paydays actually come from, and only shows up years later when the fund's companies are sold.

Venture Capitalist

Private / early-stage

Funds young private companies long before they'd ever qualify to trade on a public stock exchange.

How they use the market

A VC's money goes in years before a stock ticker exists — they buy equity directly from a startup, betting that a small number of huge winners will more than pay for the many that fail. The public stock market still matters enormously to them: an IPO (or an acquisition by a public company) is usually the "exit" that turns their paper stake into real, sellable money, so they're effectively underwriting a company's whole journey toward eventually becoming one of the tickers on a watchlist like this project's own.

In practice

Meeting founders — sourcing and pitch meetings make up a huge share of the week — deciding which few of the hundreds of pitches get a term sheet, and for portfolio companies, taking board seats and helping with hiring, strategy and the next fundraise rather than day-to-day operations.

Variations on this role

Seed-stage VC — writes the first institutional check into a company that may be just an idea and a founding team.
Growth-stage VC — writes much larger, later checks into already-proven companies to fund scaling.
Corporate VC — a big company's own venture arm, investing partly for strategic reasons (access to new tech) and partly for returns.
Angel investor — not really a firm job, but an individual doing the earliest version of this with their own money instead of a fund's.

How they're paid

A management fee on the fund (commonly 2%/year of committed capital, covering salaries and overhead) plus carried interest on profits (also commonly 20%, the same mechanic as Private Equity above) — meaning most of a VC's real payday, if it comes, is years out and depends on a small number of the fund's bets working out enormously.

Hedge Fund Manager / Portfolio Manager

Absolute return

Manages pooled money from institutions and wealthy investors, aiming to make money in both up and down markets.

How they use the market

Unlike a traditional fund that just tries to beat an index, a hedge fund manager is usually judged on absolute return — making money regardless of what the overall market does. That means being willing to bet a stock will fall (short selling) as well as rise, using derivatives to hedge or amplify bets, and often running very concentrated or leveraged positions guided by a specific strategy (long/short equity, macro, quantitative, event-driven). What matters isn't just the return, but how much risk was taken to get it.

In practice

Reviewing overnight P&L and risk first thing, then a mix of idea generation (reading, modeling, talking to management teams or industry contacts), risk conversations with the firm's risk team, and investor relations if they also run the business side of the fund.

Variations on this role

Long/short equity — the "classic" strategy: long stocks expected to rise, short ones expected to fall.
Global macro — bets on big economic themes (interest rates, currencies, commodities) rather than individual companies.
Quantitative / systematic — rules- and model-driven (overlaps with Quant above), little or no discretionary judgment.
Event-driven — trades around specific catalysts: mergers, bankruptcies, spin-offs, activist campaigns.

How they're paid

The industry-standard "2 and 20": a 2%/year management fee on assets, plus 20% of profits above a watermark — though fee pressure has pushed many funds toward smaller numbers. A PM at a multi-strategy fund is often paid a direct percentage of the P&L they personally generate instead.

Chief Financial Officer

Issuer side

Sits on the other side of the stock market from everyone else in this guide — their own company is the one being bought and sold.

How they use the market

A CFO decides how their company shows up to the market it trades on: whether to buy back stock (reducing the share count, which lifts earnings per share even if profit doesn't grow), whether to issue new shares to raise cash, how big a dividend to pay, and what guidance to give analysts about future earnings. Every one of those choices moves the same price everyone else in this guide is reacting to — which is why a public company's own finance team is, in its own way, also a stock market participant.

In practice

Running the finance organization (accounting, FP&A, treasury), presenting quarterly results to the board and to analysts on earnings calls, and making the big capital-allocation calls — buy back stock? raise the dividend? make an acquisition? pay down debt? — that this role's calculator below illustrates one version of.

Variations on this role

Public-company CFO — answers to public shareholders and SEC disclosure rules every quarter.
Private / PE-backed CFO — answers to a small set of owners instead, often with heavier debt and tighter cash management.
Startup CFO — spends more time on fundraising and burn-rate management than on public-market mechanics.

How they're paid

Salary plus an annual bonus tied to company performance, plus — especially at public companies — a large stock/option grant meant to align the CFO's own wealth with the same share price every other role in this guide is reacting to.

Key tools & concepts

Share buybacksDividend policy Earnings guidanceCapital allocation Investor relationsCredit ratings

Market Maker / Broker-Dealer

Liquidity

Stands ready to buy or sell a stock at any moment, and gets paid for providing that service, not for guessing direction.

How they use the market

A market maker continuously quotes both a price they'll buy at (the bid) and a slightly higher price they'll sell at (the ask), profiting from that small gap (the spread) across high volume rather than betting on whether a stock goes up or down. This is what makes a stock "liquid" — there's almost always someone on the other side of a trade — and the real job is risk management: managing the inventory that quoting both sides leaves you holding.

In practice

Continuously updating quotes as the market moves, managing the inventory risk that comes from being on the other side of a huge number of trades, and reacting fast to news that makes a stock's "fair value" jump before the quotes can even update.

Variations on this role

Exchange-designated market maker — has an obligation (and exchange incentives) to keep quoting a specific stock continuously, even in fast markets.
Wholesale market maker — executes retail brokerage orders directly, paying for that order flow (payment for order flow, PFOF).
Options market maker — quotes options contracts instead of (or alongside) the underlying stock, with a much bigger pricing/risk model to manage (volatility, time decay).

How they're paid

The spread they capture across huge volume (illustrated by this role's calculator), plus, for wholesalers, payment-for-order-flow revenue from brokerages. Profitability here is almost entirely about volume and risk-management discipline, not picking winners.

Equity Research Analyst

Analysis & ratings

Studies public companies in depth and publishes an opinion on whether their stock is worth buying.

How they use the market

They build detailed financial models of a company (or a whole industry), estimate what its stock is really worth, and publish a rating — Buy, Hold, or Sell — and a price target, along with the reasoning behind it. Sell-side analysts do this for outside investors and to support their bank's other business; buy-side analysts do the same work internally to inform a fund's own trades. The Strong Buy-style rating pills and conviction tiers on this project's own Market Scanner are exactly this job's output, simplified.

In practice

Building and maintaining detailed financial models for a handful of covered companies, writing notes after every earnings report, hosting or joining calls with company management, and defending (or changing) a rating when the stock moves against the thesis.

Variations on this role

Sell-side analyst — works at a bank, publishes ratings for outside clients, and supports the bank's trading/banking business (a structural conflict of interest that disclosure rules exist to manage).
Buy-side analyst — works inside a fund or asset manager, researching the same companies purely to inform that firm's own trades, with no public rating to publish.
Independent research — a smaller, subscription-funded shop with no banking relationship to create a conflict, trading broader reach for narrower resources.

How they're paid

Base salary plus a bonus tied to the quality and accuracy of their calls and, on the sell side, the trading commissions and banking business their research helps generate — a sell-side analyst's influence (and pay) often tracks how closely institutional investors follow their calls.

Risk Manager / Compliance Officer

Oversight

Doesn't pick stocks at all — makes sure everyone else at the firm doesn't blow it up.

How they use the market

A risk manager sets and enforces limits on how much any trader or portfolio can lose before someone has to answer for it, using tools like Value-at-Risk to estimate a "worst plausible day" in dollar terms, and stress tests to see what a historical shock would do to the book today. A compliance officer's job overlaps heavily: making sure trades follow securities law, client suitability rules, and insider-trading restrictions. Neither role generates profit directly, but a firm without one is one bad trade away from not existing.

In practice

Running the firm's risk reports every morning (Value-at-Risk, stress tests, concentration limits), flagging breaches before they become disasters, and — for compliance specifically — reviewing trades for insider-trading red flags, approving marketing materials, and handling regulator exams and filings.

Variations on this role

Market risk — focused on price/volatility risk across trading books (the VaR calculator below is this job).
Credit risk — focused on counterparty default risk: will the other side of a trade actually pay?
Operational risk — focused on failures in process, systems, or people (a trade booked wrong, a system outage).
Compliance — focused on following the law and the firm's own policies, rather than quantifying market risk per se.

How they're paid

Salary plus a bonus, almost always smaller than the traders and bankers they oversee — a structural tension in finance, since the people who can stop a profitable-looking trade are paid less than the people making it — though senior Chief Risk Officers at large banks are highly compensated.

Common Stock (Equity)

Ownership

A share of ownership in a single company — its value rises and falls with that one business, for better and worse.

How it works

Buying a share makes you a part-owner of that company: you're entitled to a slice of its profits (sometimes paid out as dividends) and your stake's value tracks whatever the market thinks the company is worth. Unlike a fund, every dollar of gain or loss here comes from one business's fortunes — there's no basket softening the ride.

Company size changes the risk

This site groups companies into four market-cap tiers, and size is one of the biggest single predictors of a stock's volatility:

Mega-cap ($200B+) — the largest, most liquid companies (think NVDA, AAPL, WMT). Still moves, but a single bad headline rarely swings the price more than a few percent in a day.
Large-cap ($10B–$200B) — well-established, still broadly liquid, somewhat more room to swing than mega-caps.
Mid-cap ($2B–$10B) — past the riskiest early stage but still far more sensitive to a single bad quarter than a mega-cap; this site's Funds & Company Size desk tags these names "High Risk."
Small-cap ($300M–$2B) — thinner trading, less analyst coverage, and far less room for error; a single earnings miss or contract loss can move the stock double digits in a session. Tagged "Very High Risk" on the Funds desk.

ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds)

Diversified

A single security that holds a basket of many other securities, trading on an exchange all day, just like a stock.

How it works

An ETF pools money from many investors into one fund that holds dozens, hundreds, or thousands of underlying stocks or bonds, then issues shares of itself that trade continuously on an exchange at a live price — you can buy or sell any minute the market is open, same as a stock. Because your money is spread across every holding at once, one company having a terrible day barely dents the ETF's price.

Variations on this instrument

Broad-market index ETF — tracks an entire index (e.g. the S&P 500), so it roughly mirrors "the market."
Sector or style ETF — narrows to one industry, theme, or characteristic (small-cap, dividend growers, technology), trading some diversification for a sharper bet.
Bond ETF — holds bonds instead of stocks, so it mainly reacts to interest-rate moves rather than stock-market swings.

Risk

Lower company-specific risk than owning one stock, since one holding's bad quarter is diluted across all the others — but the systematic risk of whatever it tracks doesn't go away: a broad-market ETF still falls in a broad market downturn. Expense ratios (an annual fee, often well under 0.5%/year) are the main ongoing cost.

Mutual Funds

Diversified

A pooled investment vehicle much like an ETF, but priced once a day instead of trading live all day.

How it works

Like an ETF, a mutual fund pools many investors' money into a diversified basket of holdings. The key difference: you buy and sell at the fund's Net Asset Value (NAV), calculated just once after the market closes each day — not a live, continuously-updating price. That makes it less convenient for active trading but no less diversified.

Variations on this instrument

Index mutual fund — the mutual-fund equivalent of a broad index ETF: passively tracks a benchmark, very low fees.
Actively managed fund — a professional manager picks and changes holdings trying to beat the market, usually for a noticeably higher fee; most fail to beat their index after fees over long periods.
Balanced / income fund — mixes bonds and dividend stocks in one fund for a steadier, lower-volatility ride than an all-stock fund.

Risk

Same diversification benefit as an ETF; an actively managed fund adds the manager's own strategy and judgment as an extra variable, for better or worse, on top of the market's own moves.

Bonds

Fixed income

A loan you make to a government or company, paying you interest until it repays what it borrowed.

How it works

Buying a bond means lending the issuer money for a set term in exchange for regular interest payments (the "coupon") and your principal back at maturity. Unlike a stock, you're not an owner with upside tied to the business's growth — you're a creditor, with a fixed, contractual return, as long as the issuer doesn't default.

Variations on this instrument

Treasury bonds — issued by the U.S. government, considered the safest (lowest default risk) bonds available.
Corporate bonds — issued by companies; higher yield than Treasuries to compensate for real default risk, which varies by the issuer's credit rating.
Municipal bonds — issued by states/cities, often with tax-advantaged interest for residents.

Risk

Generally far lower volatility than stocks — this is the whole reason a bond-heavy fund (like AGG or the balanced fund on this site's Funds desk) sits at the bottom of the risk ladder. But "lower risk" isn't "no risk": bond prices move opposite to interest rates (rates up, existing bond prices down), and a shakier issuer can still default.

Options (Calls & Puts)

Derivative

A contract giving you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a stock at a fixed price before a set date.

How it works

A call gives you the right to buy a stock at a set "strike" price before expiry — worth buying if you think the stock will rise above that strike. A put gives you the right to sell at the strike — worth buying if you think the stock will fall below it. You pay a premium upfront, priced (using a model like Black-Scholes) off the stock's price, the strike, time left, and the stock's own volatility; the more volatile the stock, the pricier the option.

Why it's leveraged

A small move in the underlying stock can mean a much larger percentage move in the option's value, because you're only paying for the right to the move, not the full share price. That cuts both ways: if the stock never reaches your strike, the option can expire completely worthless, and you lose the whole premium — a 100% loss is a normal, not a rare, outcome here.

Futures

Derivative

A contract obligating you to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date — unlike an option, there's no choice involved.

How it works

Both sides of a futures contract are locked in: the buyer must take the position at the agreed price, the seller must deliver it, on the agreed date. You don't pay the full value of what you're controlling upfront — you post "margin," a fraction of the contract's notional value, which creates built-in leverage: a given dollar of margin controls a much larger position than that same dollar could buy outright.

Why leverage makes this risky

Because a futures position is marked to market continuously, losses come directly out of your posted margin in real time. If losses erode your margin enough, you face a margin call: post more cash or have the position force-closed. A price move that would be a modest loss on a stock you own outright can wipe out your entire margin — or more — on a leveraged futures position.

Short Selling

Bearish strategy

Borrowing shares to sell now, betting the price falls so you can buy them back cheaper later.

How it works

You borrow shares you don't own (typically from your broker) and sell them at today's price, pocketing the proceeds. Later, you "cover" the position by buying the same number of shares back and returning them. If the price fell in between, you profit the difference; if it rose, you lose the difference. Opening a short requires margin (collateral) and costs an ongoing borrow fee for as long as the position stays open.

Why the risk is asymmetric

Buying a stock outright, the most you can ever lose is 100% of what you paid — a stock can't fall below zero. Shorting flips that: a stock can rise without any upper limit, so a short position's potential loss is theoretically unlimited. This is exactly why short sellers watch margin requirements so closely, and why a short squeeze (a sharp rally forcing short sellers to buy back at any price) can be so damaging.
