How Each Role Uses the Stock Market

The same ticker means something different depending on who's looking at it. This is a plain-language tour of how eight different finance careers actually relate to public companies and their stock — what they do day to day, and which parts of that you can practice in the Simulator.
Individual Investor Financial Advisor Private Banker Investment Banker Trader Private Equity Hedge Fund / Portfolio Manager Equity Research Analyst

Individual Investor

Retail

Manages their own money for their own goals — retirement, a house, general wealth-building.

How they use the market

They open a brokerage account and buy stocks, funds, or bonds directly with their own cash, holding a single portfolio they're fully accountable for. Decisions are usually driven by a personal time horizon and risk tolerance rather than a mandate from a client or employer, and success is just "did my net worth grow the way I needed it to."

DiversificationDollar-cost averaging Buy-and-hold vs. active tradingP/E & dividend yield Risk toleranceTax-advantaged accounts

Financial Advisor

Retail / mass affluent

Builds and manages investment plans for other people's money, tied to their clients' real-life goals.

How they use the market

Instead of picking stocks for themselves, they translate a client's goal — retire at 60, fund college in 15 years — into a target return, a risk tolerance, and an asset allocation across stocks, bonds, and cash. They rebalance over time, manage the emotional side of investing (talking clients out of panic-selling), and are usually paid a fee on assets under management rather than a commission per trade.

Key tools & concepts

Goal-based planningAsset allocation Risk tolerance questionnairesRebalancing Fee-only vs. commissionCompounding projections

Private Banker / Wealth Manager

High-net-worth

Runs several client relationships at once for wealthy individuals and families, blending investing with banking.

How they use the market

They manage a book of clients rather than one portfolio, each with a different risk profile, tax situation, and set of goals — and often a concentrated position in one stock (like a founder's or executive's own company shares) that needs careful, low-key unwinding. On top of investment management, they typically also arrange lending against a portfolio, trust and estate structures, and access to private/alternative investments not open to retail clients.

Key tools & concepts

Multi-client book managementConcentrated stock positions Securities-based lendingModel portfolios by risk profile Estate & tax planningAlternative investments

Investment Banker

Corporate finance

Advises companies — not individual investors — on raising capital and doing deals.

How they use the market

They don't manage a personal portfolio of stocks; they use the stock market as a tool for their corporate clients. That means taking companies public (IPOs), helping already-public companies issue more shares or debt, and advising on mergers and acquisitions — where a huge part of the job is figuring out whether buying another company will make the acquirer's own stock more or less valuable per share (accretion/dilution).

Key tools & concepts

DCF & comparable-company valuationPrecedent transactions Accretion / dilution analysisUnderwriting & deal structuring IPOs & follow-on offeringsPro forma modeling

Trader (Sales & Trading / Prop)

Markets & execution

Buys and sells constantly, either to fill client orders or to profit from a firm's own capital.

How they use the market

A sell-side trader sits between clients and the market, executing orders and often making markets (quoting both a buy and sell price and profiting from the spread). A proprietary trader instead risks a firm's own money on short-term views — on price direction, volatility, or the relationship between related instruments. Time horizons here are usually seconds to weeks, not years, and risk is managed trade-by-trade with hard limits (like value-at-risk), not through a long-term financial plan.

Key tools & concepts

Bid-ask spread & order flowMarket making Technical analysisLeverage & margin Value-at-risk (VaR)Execution algorithms

Private Equity Professional

Ownership & control

Buys whole companies outright — often taking them private — rather than trading shares of them.

How they use the market

Instead of buying a small slice of a company on an exchange, a PE firm buys (or takes private) an entire company using a mix of investor capital and borrowed money (a leveraged buyout), then spends several years actively improving how it's run before selling it — often back onto the public market via an IPO, which is where it reconnects with the stock market this whole site is about. Returns are measured over years, not days, using metrics like IRR and multiple of invested capital rather than daily share-price moves.

Key tools & concepts

Leveraged buyouts (LBOs)IRR & MOIC Capital structure & leverageOperational value creation Exit strategy (sale or IPO)PIPEs

Hedge Fund Manager / Portfolio Manager

Absolute return

Manages pooled money from institutions and wealthy investors, aiming to make money in both up and down markets.

How they use the market

Unlike a traditional fund that just tries to beat an index, a hedge fund manager is usually judged on absolute return — making money regardless of what the overall market does. That means being willing to bet a stock will fall (short selling) as well as rise, using derivatives to hedge or amplify bets, and often running very concentrated or leveraged positions guided by a specific strategy (long/short equity, macro, quantitative, event-driven).

Key tools & concepts

Long/short equityShort selling Derivatives & hedgingSharpe ratio LeverageQuantitative & algorithmic strategies

Equity Research Analyst

Analysis & ratings

Studies public companies in depth and publishes an opinion on whether their stock is worth buying.

How they use the market

They build detailed financial models of a company (or a whole industry), estimate what its stock is really worth, and publish a rating — Buy, Hold, or Sell — and a price target, along with the reasoning behind it. Sell-side analysts do this for outside investors and to support their bank's other business; buy-side analysts do the same work internally to inform a fund's own trades. The Strong Buy-style rating pills and conviction tiers on this project's own Market Scanner are exactly this job's output, simplified.

Key tools & concepts

DCF & comps valuationEarnings estimates Buy / Hold / Sell ratingsPrice targets Industry & competitive analysisSell-side vs. buy-side
