Common Stock (Equity)

Ownership

A share of ownership in a single company — its value rises and falls with that one business, for better and worse.

How it works

Buying a share makes you a part-owner of that company: you're entitled to a slice of its profits (sometimes paid out as dividends) and your stake's value tracks whatever the market thinks the company is worth. Unlike a fund, every dollar of gain or loss here comes from one business's fortunes — there's no basket softening the ride.

Company size changes the risk

This site groups companies into four market-cap tiers, and size is one of the biggest single predictors of a stock's volatility:

Mega-cap ($200B+) — the largest, most liquid companies (think NVDA, AAPL, WMT). Still moves, but a single bad headline rarely swings the price more than a few percent in a day.
Large-cap ($10B–$200B) — well-established, still broadly liquid, somewhat more room to swing than mega-caps.
Mid-cap ($2B–$10B) — past the riskiest early stage but still far more sensitive to a single bad quarter than a mega-cap; this site's Funds & Company Size desk tags these names "High Risk."
Small-cap ($300M–$2B) — thinner trading, less analyst coverage, and far less room for error; a single earnings miss or contract loss can move the stock double digits in a session. Tagged "Very High Risk" on the Funds desk.
