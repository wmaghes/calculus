Bonds

Fixed income

A loan you make to a government or company, paying you interest until it repays what it borrowed.

How it works

Buying a bond means lending the issuer money for a set term in exchange for regular interest payments (the "coupon") and your principal back at maturity. Unlike a stock, you're not an owner with upside tied to the business's growth — you're a creditor, with a fixed, contractual return, as long as the issuer doesn't default.

Variations on this instrument

Treasury bonds — issued by the U.S. government, considered the safest (lowest default risk) bonds available.
Corporate bonds — issued by companies; higher yield than Treasuries to compensate for real default risk, which varies by the issuer's credit rating.
Municipal bonds — issued by states/cities, often with tax-advantaged interest for residents.

Risk

Generally far lower volatility than stocks — this is the whole reason a bond-heavy fund (like AGG or the balanced fund on this site's Funds desk) sits at the bottom of the risk ladder. But "lower risk" isn't "no risk": bond prices move opposite to interest rates (rates up, existing bond prices down), and a shakier issuer can still default.
