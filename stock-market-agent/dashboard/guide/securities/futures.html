Futures

Derivative

A contract obligating you to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date — unlike an option, there's no choice involved.

How it works

Both sides of a futures contract are locked in: the buyer must take the position at the agreed price, the seller must deliver it, on the agreed date. You don't pay the full value of what you're controlling upfront — you post "margin," a fraction of the contract's notional value, which creates built-in leverage: a given dollar of margin controls a much larger position than that same dollar could buy outright.

Why leverage makes this risky

Because a futures position is marked to market continuously, losses come directly out of your posted margin in real time. If losses erode your margin enough, you face a margin call: post more cash or have the position force-closed. A price move that would be a modest loss on a stock you own outright can wipe out your entire margin — or more — on a leveraged futures position.
